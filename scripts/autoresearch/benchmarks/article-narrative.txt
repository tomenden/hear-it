# title: The First-Person Industrial Complex
# publication: Slate
# author: Laura Bennett
# published: September 14, 2015

A few months ago, Natasha Chenier submitted a piece to Jezebel about her sexual relationship with her dad. She described meeting her biological father for the first time at age 19 and being gradually overtaken by lust for him. Writing that essay was "terrifying," but it felt inevitable. Chenier had always kept a journal and had been reading Jezebel for years. "I had this story I'd always wanted to tell," she says, "and suddenly I felt like the world was ready."

First-person writing has long been the Internet's native voice. Young people have consistently scraped their interior lives to convert raw experiences into copy. Currently, we're in an unprecedented moment in the online first-person boom. The rise of unreported hot takes has meant editors constantly search for writers with any expertise on a topic to elevate their pieces above the swarm. First-person essays have become the easiest way to stake out corners of news stories without paying for reporting, and they're the easiest way to jolt an increasingly jaded Internet to attention.

It's been seven years since former Gawker editor Emily Gould appeared on the New York Times Magazine cover. Since then, the Internet's confessional impulse has been fully codified. Every site seems to have a first-person vertical and editor. Early adopters like Gawker, Jezebel, and xoJane jostle for attention alongside BuzzFeed Ideas, PostEverything, and Vox First Person. Yet all the headlines tend to blur together, whether they're Vox's scannable sections or BuzzFeed's casual tone.

Despite cataloging wide-ranging hardship, these essays share a tendency to reach for universality while digging into acutely personal experiences. A Vox essay titled "How I Came to Forgive My Rapist" ends with "All that endures now is my wish for an end to rape for everyone else." XoJane's piece about infidelity declares "I hope someone can learn from my mistakes." These essays dress up personal narratives in the language of the political.

This pressure to ensure every story finds an ardent audience wasn't nearly so intense in earlier eras. "Back then," Gould says, "I was just writing about myself because it was the thing I knew how to do. I was not thinking about traffic." The old school of first-person Internet writers built relationships with readers through self-exposure, cultivating boundary-pushing personas. Modern iterations often feel like one-offs — solo acts of sensational disclosure that bubble up and quickly vaporize.

Rebecca Carroll, formerly an editor at xoJane, recalls reading submissions that were "pretty tone deaf, just totally un-self-aware." She published one anyway, knowing it would be provocative, though "There was an enormous backlash, and the writer was traumatized." Many recent essays make shows of maximal divulgence but are too half-baked to do real introspection work.

The new first-person economy incentivizes knee-jerk, topical self-exposure. The mandate at xoJane was that the more "shameless" an essay, the better. Yet this creates what Tolentino describes: "a situation in which writers feel like the best thing they have to offer is the worst thing that ever happened to them." The speed of the churn is grueling, and editors struggle weighing ethics against market forces demanding these pieces.
